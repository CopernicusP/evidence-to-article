# Grounded authorial voice

Use this reference for explanatory articles, blogs and other formats where the task calls for a visible author. Keep the requested genre: a personal voice is an option, not a requirement for every report or every language.

## Select a voice for the run

Record the audience, narrator and purpose in the brief. Choose a fitting mode, such as:

- **Neutral explainer:** clear analysis with little visible narrator.
- **Grounded personal:** visible editorial judgment, natural direct address and an individual rhythm.
- **Institutional commentary:** an organization's supplied position and professional voice.

These are editorial choices read by the agent, not CLI switches. Do not infer an organization's position from its logo or a past episode. Use the current request before profile defaults. Do not force the Chinese and English outputs to use the same narrator.

## Put personality into decisions

Pick the detail that makes the subject worth explaining. State what deserves attention and show why the evidence supports that focus. Order definitions and background around the reader's developing understanding; do not make every research finding equally prominent.

Let the reader see a useful inference, surprise or tradeoff. Explain the step connecting evidence to judgment, especially when a compact technical sentence leaves that step unstated. Use first person when it expresses present analysis, selection or a proposed method. Do not add a pronoun to every paragraph.

Preserve appropriate humor, hesitation, attitude and varied rhythm when they contribute meaning. Do not prescribe a quota of jokes, short sentences, questions or catchphrases. Address the material with the reader; avoid a tone that treats the reader as foolish.

Say what a document or observation establishes before exploring its limits. Keep necessary qualifications beside the relevant claim. Move lookup identifiers and optional procedural detail to notes when they interrupt the argument. Stop after resolving the opening question; add an organizational application only when it serves the actual audience.

## Keep the evidence boundary

First-person interpretation is not firsthand reporting. “I would compare the dates next” describes a method; “I spoke with the participants” asserts an event and requires evidence. Do not invent memories, interviews, investments, tests, emotional reactions or other people's motives. Do not borrow a reference author's identity or distinctive stories.

“I think” does not make an unsupported allegation acceptable. Check the proposition that follows it. Preserve scope, source attribution, legal status, time, amount, conditions and uncertainty. A stronger tone must not turn a plan into an outcome, a possibility into a certainty, or a narrowly qualified conclusion into a universal one.

An analogy can clarify a relationship but cannot establish a fact or legal equivalence. A personal voice may remain measured; dramatic language is not evidence of authorship or quality.

## Review the passage

Ask a fresh reader to identify the opening question, the author's useful judgment and the part that felt slow. Request exact passages and reasons, not a score or a count of “human” words.

Check whether each heading and section adds a new step rather than classifying another topic. Ask where a reader must supply missing reasoning, and where the ending repeats a conclusion already reached. Repair those passages before adding conversational fillers.

In a separate source review, inspect meaning changed by the voice edit. For example, “may arrive later” must not become “always arrives later.” Retain a real reader's feedback with its date, artifact and scope. One person's positive response supports that person's preference; it does not demonstrate universal improvement, organizational approval or lower editing time.

## Synthetic example

The following notice and rewrites are invented teaching material, not reporting:

Evidence: A library has approved a four-week trial extending Friday closing from 6 p.m. to 8 p.m. The trial has not begun. The number of seats will stay the same. Demand will be evaluated afterward.

Flat version: “The extended operating hours improve service availability. The trial arrangement and capacity conditions should be considered together.”

Grounded personal version: “What catches my attention is the extra two hours on Friday. That would give an evening reader more time, but the library is not adding seats. I'd want to see the demand figures after the four-week trial before calling the room less crowded.”

中文示例：“我更在意周五多出来的这两个小时：试行时读者晚上能多待一会儿，座位却不会增加。至于是否更容易找到座位，还得等四周试行后的使用情况。”

The judgment selects a useful comparison without pretending the author visited the library or knows how the trial will turn out. Full bilingual examples and the notice belong in the repository's synthetic example, not a client's evidence packet.
